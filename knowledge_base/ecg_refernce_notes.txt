ECG INTERPRETATION REFERENCE NOTES
(Original summary material written for the CardioAgent knowledge base.
Covers general electrocardiography concepts and the five PTB-XL
diagnostic superclasses. This is educational reference material for a
research prototype, not clinical guidance.)

SECTION 1: BASIC ECG WAVEFORM COMPONENTS

The electrocardiogram records the heart's electrical activity as a
sequence of repeating waveforms. The P wave marks depolarization of the
atria, the heart's upper chambers, and normally appears as a small,
rounded deflection preceding each heartbeat. The QRS complex follows and
represents depolarization of the ventricles, the heart's larger lower
chambers; it is usually the most prominent, sharpest feature of the
waveform because ventricular muscle mass is much greater than atrial
muscle mass. The ST segment is the flat portion between the end of the
QRS complex and the start of the T wave, representing the period when the
ventricles are fully depolarized; its position relative to the baseline
is closely examined because deviations can indicate reduced blood supply
to heart muscle. The T wave represents ventricular repolarization, the
electrical recovery phase after each contraction.

The PR interval, measured from the start of the P wave to the start of
the QRS complex, reflects how long it takes electrical activity to travel
from the atria through the AV node to the ventricles. The QT interval,
measured from the start of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave,
reflects the total duration of ventricular electrical activity and is
sensitive to heart rate, medications, and electrolyte levels.

Heart rate variability, or the beat-to-beat variation in timing between
consecutive heartbeats, is influenced by the autonomic nervous system and
can be reduced in various cardiac and non-cardiac conditions, though low
variability alone is not diagnostic of any single condition.

SECTION 2: NORMAL ECG (NORM)

A normal ECG typically originates from the heart's natural pacemaker, the
sinus node, producing a regular rhythm with evenly spaced beats. Each P
wave is followed by a QRS complex at a consistent, expected interval, and
the overall waveform shape remains stable from one beat to the next. The
ST segment sits at the same level as the baseline between beats, without
significant elevation or depression, and the T wave points in the
expected direction relative to the QRS complex. A normal ECG does not by
itself rule out all forms of heart disease, since some conditions produce
symptoms without any resting ECG abnormality, but it is a reassuring
baseline finding in the absence of other concerning clinical signs.

SECTION 3: MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION (MI)

Myocardial infarction occurs when blood flow to a portion of heart muscle
is significantly reduced or blocked, typically due to a blockage in a
coronary artery, leading to tissue damage if not promptly treated. On the
ECG, acute myocardial infarction can produce ST-segment elevation in the
leads corresponding to the affected region of the heart, since different
ECG leads are positioned to reflect electrical activity from different
anatomical areas. In other cases, particularly with less severe or
non-blockage-related reductions in blood flow, ST-segment depression or
T-wave inversion may be seen instead of elevation. Over hours to days,
if the affected tissue is damaged extensively enough, pathological Q
waves may develop and can persist long-term as a marker of prior
infarction. Because ECG changes evolve over time, a single ECG capturing
one moment does not always show the full pattern, and comparison with
prior or follow-up ECGs, along with blood tests for cardiac injury
markers, is typically part of a full clinical evaluation.

SECTION 4: ST/T-WAVE CHANGE (STTC)

ST/T-wave changes refer to abnormalities in the ST segment or T wave that
do not, on their own, necessarily indicate an acute infarction. Such
changes can arise from reduced blood flow that falls short of causing
tissue damage (ischemia without infarction), electrolyte imbalances such
as abnormal potassium or calcium levels, certain medications including
some antiarrhythmic and psychiatric drugs, or other systemic conditions
affecting the heart's electrical recovery phase. Because these changes
are frequently nonspecific, meaning they can arise from multiple
different underlying causes, they are generally interpreted together with
a patient's symptoms, history, and other test results rather than in
isolation.

SECTION 5: CONDUCTION DISTURBANCE (CD)

Conduction disturbances involve delays or interruptions in how electrical
signals travel through the heart's specialized conduction pathways. These
pathways include the AV node, which relays signals from the atria to the
ventricles, and the bundle branches, which distribute the signal
throughout the ventricular muscle. A delay or block at the AV node can
lengthen the PR interval or, in more severe cases, cause some or all
atrial signals to fail to reach the ventricles. A block in one of the
bundle branches typically widens the QRS complex and changes its shape,
because the affected side of the heart must be activated through a slower,
indirect electrical pathway instead of the normal fast conduction system.
Some conduction disturbances are incidental findings with little clinical
significance, while others can affect heart rhythm and function
significantly, so clinical context again plays a large role in
interpretation.

SECTION 6: HYPERTROPHY (HYP)

Hypertrophy refers to thickening of heart muscle tissue, most often
affecting the walls of the ventricles. It commonly develops as an
adaptive response to conditions that increase the workload on the heart
over time, such as long-standing high blood pressure or certain valve
abnormalities. Because a thicker muscle wall generates more electrical
voltage during depolarization, hypertrophy is often inferred on the ECG
from increased QRS amplitude in specific lead combinations, along with
possible changes in the heart's overall electrical axis and secondary
ST/T-wave changes. ECG-based criteria for hypertrophy are useful
screening indicators but are considered less precise than direct imaging
methods such as echocardiography, which can measure wall thickness
directly.

SECTION 7: GENERAL PRINCIPLES FOR INTERPRETING AUTOMATED ECG ANALYSIS

Automated ECG analysis systems, including machine learning models,
identify statistical patterns in waveform data associated with particular
labels during training. A model's prediction reflects patterns it learned
from its training data and does not constitute an independent clinical
judgment. Model outputs are most usefully understood as one input among
several that a clinician would weigh, alongside patient history, symptoms,
physical examination findings, and other diagnostic tests, rather than as
a stand-alone diagnostic conclusion. This is particularly important for
research prototypes evaluated on a limited dataset and patient population,
where performance on other populations, equipment, or clinical settings
has not been established.
